# Writing Style for Presentations

## General Principles

- **Lead with the point**: Open with the main conclusion or question. Make titles convey specific information rather than generic section labels.
- **Be specific**: Name the subject, action, and result. Replace vague language such as "empower," "leverage," and "synergy" with facts and details. Avoid hype.
- **Write concisely**: Keep each sentence focused on one idea. Remove repetition, empty modifiers, and unnecessary setup while preserving conditions and explanations needed for understanding.
- **Make the reasoning clear**: Support claims with evidence and explain causes, tradeoffs, and scope. Distinguish facts, goals, and assumptions; do not present speculation as an established conclusion.
- **Use consistent terminology**: Explain concepts and abbreviations according to the audience's knowledge. Use the same name for the same concept throughout.
- **Match detail to purpose**: Make text for spoken presentations easy to scan; preserve complete arguments in materials intended for reading. Avoid rigid word limits.
- **Make recommendations actionable**: State the action, expected outcome, and necessary conditions. For collaborative work, specify owners and deadlines. Avoid closing with slogans.

## Management Reports

- Use professional language appropriate to the presenter's role. Titles should state performance, causes, or requests so readers can follow the report by scanning its titles alone.
- Example title: "Q3 gross margin fell 3.2 percentage points, mainly due to higher raw material costs," rather than "Business Performance Report."
- Present results alongside their comparison baselines. Do not exaggerate achievements or omit unfavorable facts.
- Give actions a priority, owner, and deadline. Explain the expected impact and alternatives when requesting resources.

## Technical and Engineering Presentations

- State conclusions directly. Avoid suspense or saving the answer for a final reveal.
- Follow the decision sequence: goals and constraints → current state and bottlenecks → mechanisms and evidence → tradeoffs → rollout and rollback → risks and next steps.
- Use text to explain motivations, tradeoffs, and evidence; use diagrams to show call relationships and data flow. Avoid repeating the same information in both.
- Ground broad descriptions such as "high availability," "high concurrency," and "cloud-native" in specific SLAs, QPS figures, protocols, or components. Avoid marketing language such as "revolutionary," "redefining," and "disruptive."
- For performance claims, state the environment, versions, sample size, and load conditions. Use appropriate percentiles or confidence intervals rather than reporting only averages. Distinguish the current state, targets, assumptions, and verified conclusions.
- Close with a path to adoption or implementation, rather than only "Thank you" or an unsupported slogan.

## Analysis and Decision Support

- Work from conclusions toward evidence: state the judgment in the title, then develop supporting claims that each stand on their own and have verifiable evidence.
- Explain chart findings, the claims they support, their implications for decisions, and the data definitions and limitations.
- Preserve depth in materials intended for reading. Make conclusions easy to scan and evidence available for close reading; do not impose the minimal text requirements of a launch presentation.

## Business Proposals and Launches

- **Persuasive proposals**: Develop the argument through pain points → solution and advantages → value. Support each advantage with data, a case study, or a comparison. Close with the action you want the audience to take.
- **Launch presentations**: Present one idea per slide, aiming for a single line of body text. Favor images and short sentences. A possible narrative is interest → conflict → solution reveal → significance.
- Choose the approach for the audience and purpose. Avoid applying launch-style suspense and minimal copy to proposals that require substantial evidence.

## Education and Training

- Use titles to pose learning questions or state conclusions. Give learners definitions or conclusions they can restate. When prompting thought, pose the question before developing the answer.
- Express learning objectives as observable actions, such as "explain the cause," "compare approaches," or "complete the procedure," rather than simply "understand," "master," or "be familiar with."
- Define new concepts before explaining their boundaries, components, or counterexamples. Do not introduce terms only in figure captions.
- Show the reasoning process, key tradeoffs, and common mistakes, not just the final answer.
- Motivate learning with real situations and connect it to practice through tasks, checklists, or exercises. Section summaries should answer the section's questions rather than repeat every slide title.
